# Trade-Wind Isles pilot: research and cultural-review gate

## Scope of this milestone

This is a fictional, mechanics-only world-pack pilot. It is not a claim that there is one interchangeable “Caribbean style.” The current implementation changes water depth, reef shelves, vessel passages, shallow-water foundations, rain collection, prevailing-wind details, schedules, business presentation, and expedition rewards. It deliberately does **not** add final music, clothing, cuisine, language, resident naming, or a new formation/story catalogue.

Before any of those cultural layers are authored, the pack needs a narrower geographic and community frame and review by people with lived or professional expertise in that place. Until then, all current names and presentation strings are provisional.

## Focused source notes

- NOAA’s regional material describes Caribbean basins, shallow banks, inter-bank depressions, and named inter-island passages. This supports a legible shallow-shelf/deep-passage navigation mechanic, not a universal settlement form: <https://www.coris.noaa.gov/activities/deepcoral_rpt/Chapter8_Caribbean.pdf>
- NOAA’s coastal study of northeastern Cuba describes fringing reefs, very shallow bays, coral heads, reinforced trade winds, and relatively few deep-water entrance channels. The pilot’s reef shelves and constrained vessel corridors are grounded in that specific coastal pattern: <https://repository.library.noaa.gov/view/noaa/35487/noaa_35487_DS1.pdf>
- NOAA research around Bonaire distinguishes leeward reef systems on islands dominated by trade winds. This is why wind direction is saved as pack data and affects schedules and visible cloth rather than acting as a generic tropical decoration: <https://repository.library.noaa.gov/view/noaa/23825>
- UNESCO’s tentative-list record for the Ciénaga Grande de Santa Marta documents a particular Colombian Caribbean stilt-building tradition, including wind-oriented roofs, docks, waterborne travel, and construction as shared community knowledge. Stilt foundations are therefore treated as an environmental building rule for the shallow-water pilot, while the source’s specific cultural forms are not copied into a pan-Caribbean aesthetic: <https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/5842/>
- The U.S. National Park Service documents rainwater collection through architecture in San Juan and other Caribbean historic sites. The pilot abstracts this into a visible courtyard catchment rather than borrowing a specific fort or domestic style: <https://www.nps.gov/saju/learn/historyculture/san-cristobal.htm> and <https://www.nps.gov/places/000/water-cistern.htm>

## Required informed review before cultural content

1. Select a narrower island, coast, or connected maritime region instead of expanding a composite Caribbean visual language.
2. Invite at least one architectural or material-culture specialist and one artist, historian, or community practitioner connected to that place.
3. Review the pack title, building/business names, plants, vessel types, colors/materials, resident presentation, and the historical implications of trade and harbor imagery.
4. Treat colonial infrastructure and trade history explicitly and carefully; do not turn it into placeless picturesque scenery.
5. Record reviewer feedback and resulting changes here before adding music, clothing, cuisine, language, or authored GROW stories.
